> When you sit across from someone outside your group, a client, another team, a landlord, you are the group in that moment. To them, you and the people beside you are one thing, and how united you seem is how solid the group looks. Speaking with one voice is a skill, and it mostly comes down to sorting things out among yourselves first, so the outsider meets a team.

## Why the united front matters

The person on the other side reads the whole group through whoever is in front of them. If two of you openly disagree about basic facts, or one contradicts another, or someone reopens a question the group already settled, the outsider learns something worrying: this group is not actually coordinated. Trust drops, because they can no longer tell who speaks for the group or which answer to rely on.

Real disagreement is fine, and often necessary. It just belongs in the right room. Work it out among yourselves first, then bring the outsider one position they can count on.

## Keep the questions inside the team

A common slip is asking a teammate something, in front of the client, that should have been settled in private. "Wait, why are we even doing it this way?" said across the table tells the outsider the group does not know its own plan. If a real question comes up, hold it, and raise it with your team out of the room, or route it through whoever is leading.

If you are not the one running the conversation, follow their lead. Speak when it helps, keep it short, and let the person who owns the relationship steer. Jumping in at the wrong moment, or talking over the lead, breaks the single voice the group is trying to present.

## Sort it out, then speak

Before any conversation where you stand in for a group, get aligned: who leads, what the group's position is, what is still open. During it, keep private questions private and let the lead lead. Afterward, take the loose ends back to the team. Do this and the people across from you walk away with a simple, valuable impression, that they are dealing with one coordinated group they can trust, which is what a group should feel like from the outside.
