> Any goal you chase has smaller goals underneath it. Win the game, so reach a winning position, so build strength on the board, so take a piece. The smaller goals are useful. They are also a trap, because it is easy to chase one so hard that you forget the goal it was meant to serve. The skill is holding the top goal in view the whole time, and dropping a smaller one the moment it stops serving the bigger one.

## Goals sit inside bigger goals

Think of the goals in a chess game, stacked from the top down.

- Win the game.
- Reach a position that is likely to win.
- Build strength on the board.
- Capture the other player's pieces.

Each lower goal exists only to serve the one above it. Capturing pieces is good because it usually builds strength, and strength usually leads to a winning position, and that wins the game. A good player watches the lower goals but never forgets the top one.

## Give up the small goal for the big one

New players often do one thing above all: they grab as many of the other player's pieces as they can. Each piece feels like progress, so they take every one on offer.

A stronger player thinks differently. They will give away a piece, even a powerful one, if losing it leads to a winning position. They sacrifice the small goal, taking pieces, to serve the top goal, winning. Whole games turn on one player understanding that the lower goal was never the real point.

That is the test whenever two of your goals collide. Ask which one sits higher. Then serve the higher one, even if it means letting the lower one go.

## Measure the thing that actually matters

There is a quieter version of this trap. You react to a number because it is easy to see, when a different number is the one that decides the outcome.

Someone once argued that a screen had to load its data in small pages, because the response held twelve thousand items. Twelve thousand sounds like far too much to send at once. But for loading the data, the count was never the point. The weight was. Those twelve thousand items added up to about 125 kilobytes, a small fraction of one photo on the same screen, and small enough to arrive in one go without anyone noticing. The scary-looking number, the count, was not the one that decided how fast the data loaded.

So before you react to a number, ask whether it is the number that actually decides the result. Often the loud one is not.

## Ask what you're really after

When you feel stuck or pulled two ways, stop and name the top goal out loud, the thing the task in front of you is meant to serve. Then check whether what you are about to do serves it. Most bad decisions are a lower goal quietly taking the place of a higher one, and just naming the higher one puts it back in charge.
