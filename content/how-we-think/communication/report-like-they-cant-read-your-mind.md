> When you finish something, or hit a snag, the other person knows only what you tell them. A one-word "done" carries almost no information. The habit worth building is to tell them enough, in that first message, that they can act without coming back to ask you anything.

## "Done" leaves them guessing

A message that just says "done" hands all the work of understanding to the reader. Done how? Where do I see it? Is it safe to use? A useful note answers those before they are asked. Three things cover most of it:

- What is finished.
- Where to see or try it.
- How to check that it works.

"Finished the signup form. It is live on the test site at this link. Try creating an account with any email, and it should land on the welcome page." That is a message someone can act on right away. It took twenty extra seconds to write and saves several rounds of questions.

## What you leave out, they cannot know

The reverse is more dangerous. If you know something and do not say it, the other person has no way to reach it. They cannot read your mind, and they will act as if the unsaid thing does not exist.

Take a common pattern. The job was to remove something the system should no longer depend on. It got removed in one place, but a copy was left in another. The person who spotted the leftover said nothing, assuming it was handled. Much later, that exact leftover caused a failure. One sentence at the time, "I took it out here, but there is still a copy over there," would have prevented all of it. The information existed. It just stayed in one person's head.

## Assume they know only what you wrote

Before you send an update, read it back as the other person will, someone who can see only these words and nothing inside your head. Ask whether they could act on it without a single follow-up, and whether anything you know but did not write would change what they do. If so, add it. The cost is a sentence. The cost of leaving it out can be everything built on top of the gap.
