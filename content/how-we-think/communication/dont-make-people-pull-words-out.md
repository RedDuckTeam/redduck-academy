      of the task.

> Good communication has a simple test. After you say something, does the other person have to ask a follow-up just to understand you? If they do, you made them pull the words out. The aim is to say it fully enough the first time that no pulling is needed.

## The tell: they have to ask you again

Someone says they will finish a task today. The next day, another person has to ask, "so, did you finish it?" That question is already a small sign that something went wrong. The update should have arrived on its own.

Worse is the answer that sometimes follows: "yeah, ages ago." So it was done, and nobody thought to say so. Everyone was left guessing while the one person who knew stayed quiet. Finishing the work is only half the job. Letting the right people know is the other half, and it should not wait for them to come asking.

## Short answers make people dig

The same problem shows up in the middle of a conversation. Someone asks you to explain something, and you answer in three or four words that do not carry the whole thought. So they ask another question. You give another fragment. It can take five rounds to get out what one full answer would have covered.

Each round costs both of you time and attention, and it slowly wears down the person asking. Handing over the whole thought at once is faster, clearer, and far easier to be on the receiving end of.

## Say the whole thing the first time

Before you send a short reply, ask what the other person will need next, and put it in now. If they will want to know when, say when. If they will ask where to look, include the link. Picture the follow-up questions your message would set off, and answer them before they are asked. Done well, a whole conversation shrinks to a single message.
