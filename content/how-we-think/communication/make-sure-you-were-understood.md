> Sending a message is not the same as being understood, and the space between the two is where most miscommunication hides. Closing it takes a few cheap habits: confirm what landed, write down what was decided, and check things from the other side before you rely on them.

## Confirm, don't assume it landed

After an important conversation, do not assume everyone walked away with the same picture. Confirm it. The fastest way is to have the other person say back what they are going to do, in their own words. If they say it correctly, you both know it landed. If they say something slightly off, you just caught a mistake in ten seconds instead of a week.

This has nothing to do with distrust. Two people leave the same conversation with different pictures far more often than they expect, and a quick check catches it. A simple "so, to be clear, you'll do X and I'll do Y" saves an enormous amount of later confusion.

## Write it down so you don't ask twice

If something matters, write it where you will find it again. What was decided, what the answer was, what you promised. Memory leaks, and re-asking a question you already asked has a real cost. It wastes the other person's time, and it quietly tells them the first answer went nowhere.

Someone who asks a client the same question on two calls in a row does more than waste a minute. They signal that the earlier answer was not kept. A ten-second note after the first call prevents that completely.

## Check it works from the other side

When you hand someone a link, a file, or access to something, what works on your screen may not work on theirs. You already have permission. They may not. Before you rely on it, check it from their side, or ask them to confirm they can actually open it.

This is a tiny step that heads off a whole category of stalls, where one person thinks they have shared something and the other is quietly stuck, unable to get in.

## Close the loop

Treat a message as delivered only once it has actually been understood, rather than the moment you hit send. For anything that matters, close the loop: have the plan said back, keep a note of what was agreed, and confirm the other person can do their part. The few seconds each of these costs are nothing next to the days a quiet misunderstanding can eat.
