> Here is a test that catches a surprising share of bad decisions before they cost anything. Take the plan you are about to commit to and ask whether it would make sense in the real world, with real people acting in their own interest. If the plan would be absurd played out by actual humans, it is absurd on paper too. The check takes ten seconds and saves hours.

## Run the situation in your head first

Most mistakes look fine in the abstract and turn ridiculous the moment you picture real people living them out. So picture them. Before you commit to a plan, run it as a short story with ordinary, self-interested people in the roles, and watch what happens.

You take out a loan and put up your car as security. Then you default, and everyone knows you cannot repay. In your plan, the lender shrugs and lets you keep the car, free and clear. Play that out as a real scene and it falls apart at once. The lender's whole protection was the right to take the car and sell it if you failed to pay. A plan where they walk away and leave it with you does not match a world with rational people in it, so something in the plan is wrong.

## Assume some of the people are adversarial

"Makes sense in the real world" has a second half. The real world contains people who will act in their own interest even when it costs you, so the check has to include someone trying to take advantage.

Imagine a currency exchange that accepts only dollars and euros. The clerk verifies your money against a list of accepted currencies before taking it. The flaw is who holds the list: you do. You walk in, hand over a currency you invented this morning along with a list that happens to include it, and the clerk, seeing it on the list, accepts it. Absurd, obviously. Nobody lets the customer supply the list of what counts as real money.

Yet the same shape shows up constantly in plans and systems: the thing being checked is also allowed to supply the rules of the check. Stated as a real-world scene, the absurdity is impossible to miss. Buried in an abstract plan, it slips right through.

## What to do when it fails the check

The check is only useful if failing it changes your next move. When you run the story and it comes out ridiculous, that is the signal to stop and ask, before you build anything. Maybe you misunderstood the task. Maybe the plan really is broken. Either way the cost of asking now is one question. The cost of finding out later is everything you built on top of the mistake.

## Before you build, tell the story

Before you act on any plan of more than trivial size, tell it to yourself as a story about real people. Give them ordinary motives, and give at least one of them a reason to cheat. If the story holds together, go ahead. If any part of it makes you wince because no real person would behave that way, you have found the part to fix. This single habit will catch more errors than any amount of careful checking after the fact.
