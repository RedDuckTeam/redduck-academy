  - question: What is the difference between being problem-oriented and solution-oriented?
    answer: A problem-oriented person spends their first effort describing why
      something is hard and what they would need to solve it. A solution-oriented
      person spends the same effort proposing and testing an actual solution. The
      difference is usually the direction of attention in the first minute rather
      than a gap in ability or knowledge.
  - question: Isn't it careless to jump to a solution before fully understanding the problem?
    answer: Proposing a rough solution early is itself a way of understanding the
      problem, because a concrete proposal is easy to test and correct. It does not
      mean ignoring the problem, only refusing to stop at describing it. For large
      or risky problems you still think first, but you think toward a solution.
  - question: When is it actually right to ask for another specialist?
    answer: When you have tried to find or build a solution yourself and hit a real
      limit. Needing help is a conclusion you reach after an honest attempt.
      Treating it as the starting point is how ordinary tasks get turned into
      blockers.

> A problem lands on you, and the very first move decides everything after it. You can start by mapping the problem: its causes, everything that makes it hard, everyone you might need. Or you can start by reaching for a solution, even a rough one. The two openings feel the same for about thirty seconds and then split apart completely. This is the one habit the rest of the course is built on. Reach for the solution first.

## Two ways to spend fifteen minutes

Picture two people handed the same ordinary task. The first spends fifteen minutes explaining it. Why it is harder than it looks, what the root causes are, which specialist or tool or extra pair of hands would be needed to do it properly. Every sentence is true. None of it moves the task forward.

The second person spends the same fifteen minutes solving it. Sometimes they finish. Sometimes they come back with "here is how I would do it, I will have it done by the afternoon." Either way, at the end of the fifteen minutes one person is holding a plan and the other is holding a description.

The gap between them is almost never knowledge. It is the direction they pointed their attention in the first minute. Problems and solutions are both bottomless. You can always find more to say about why something is hard. The solution-oriented person just refuses to spend their opening minutes there.

## Start from a guess at the answer

The practical version of this is small. When a problem arrives, make your first output a candidate solution, however rough. "I think we can do X" is a completely different starting point from "the issue is X, and also Y, and I am not sure about Z."

A candidate solution can be wrong and still be worth more than a perfect description. It gives everyone something concrete to react to, and reacting to a concrete proposal is far faster than assembling one from a pile of described difficulties. If the guess is wrong, the correction is usually quick. If it is right, you are already most of the way done.

Two examples, both tiny once you see them:

- A group spent twenty minutes on what should happen when someone signs up with an email that already has an account. The entire answer was one line of text on the screen: "Create a new account, or sign in to your existing one." No meeting required. The solution was smaller than the discussion about it.
- Someone had a client who kept misreading their messages because the two did not share a language. You could write a paragraph about that problem. The solution was to run each message through a translator before sending it. Copy, paste, done.

Neither of these needed a specialist. They needed one person to point at the answer instead of the difficulty.

## When you really are stuck

None of this means every problem gives way to a rough guess, or that you never need anyone's help. Sometimes a task genuinely needs another person, and sometimes you cannot see a solution no matter how you turn it. Those cases are real. They are also rare. Most of what gets narrated as a wall is an ordinary task waiting for someone to begin it. So treat "I need someone else" as a conclusion you reach after trying, and let the trying come first.

## Train the first minute

The next time something lands on you and your first instinct is to explain why it is hard, notice the instinct and turn it around. Spend the opening minute writing down a possible solution instead. Even a bad one. You are training the direction your attention points when a problem shows up, and that direction, repeated a few hundred times, is most of what people mean when they call someone resourceful.
