> You can learn a hundred separate facts, or you can learn the one rule that produces all hundred. The second is less to remember and worth far more, because it also covers the facts you have not seen yet. Whenever you catch yourself memorizing a list, stop and look for the principle underneath it. Usually there is one, and once you have it the list becomes obvious.

## A hundred facts or one rule

Suppose you set out to memorize that one times one is one, two times one is two, three times one is three, and so on up the line. You could fill a page and still not be finished, because the list never ends. Or you could learn a single fact: any number multiplied by one is itself. Now you know every entry on that endless list, including the ones you never wrote down.

That is the whole idea in miniature. A principle packs many facts into the single rule that produces them. Facts are cheap to state and expensive to store, and there are always more of them. A principle costs a little more effort to grasp and then pays you back every time a new case appears, because you can work out the answer instead of looking it up.

## Why the principle covers what the facts cannot

A list of facts only ever covers the cases you already collected. The moment a new one appears, the list is silent and you are stuck. A principle does not have that gap. It answers for cases nobody thought to write down, because it describes the reason the answers come out the way they do.

This is exactly how one of the most thorough kinds of software testing works. Instead of checking a handful of specific inputs and hoping the rest behave, you state a rule the code must always obey, then let a machine throw thousands of random inputs at it to try to break that rule. You are testing the principle, so a single statement covers cases you would never have listed by hand. The same move works far outside code. Understand why a sauce holds together and you can fix any sauce, instead of following ten separate recipes and being helpless with the eleventh.

## When a list feels endless

When you are learning something and it feels like an endless pile of separate things to remember, treat that feeling as a signal that you have not found the principle yet. Ask what rule would make all these facts follow automatically. Look for the one sentence that, once you believe it, makes the rest predictable. You will remember less, understand more, and handle the case that never made it onto anyone's list.
