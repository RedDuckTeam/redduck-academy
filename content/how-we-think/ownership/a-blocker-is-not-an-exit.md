> An obstacle on the way to a task is a smaller problem to solve on the way to the bigger one. It is never permission to drop the task. What separates people here is what they do in the first minutes after the first path is blocked.

## The task outlives a failed attempt

Someone is asked to reach a particular person and pass along some information. They run into a snag. Maybe they contact the wrong person by the same name, maybe they get no reply. And then the task simply vanishes from their mind. Three days later, asked about it, they say only "I couldn't find them," as if that closed the matter.

It did not. The task was never "send one message and see what happens." It was "get the information to that person." The first attempt failing did not finish the task, and it did not cancel it. It just meant the first path was blocked and a second one was needed.

## There is always another path

A blocked path is one path. Look at everything still open:

- No reply to your message? Send a short follow-up.
- Still nothing? Suggest a specific time to talk, or put a meeting on their calendar.
- Wrong person? Ask them who the right one is, or ask someone who would know.

The point is that "the obvious way did not work" is the beginning of problem-solving. It is a long way from the end. People who get things done treat a blocker as one closed road with others still open.

## Find the next path

When your first attempt stalls, do not let the task quietly disappear off your list. Stop and ask what other routes exist, because there are almost always several. Pick the next one and take it. A task is finished when the goal is reached, and a single blocked path is just one of many ways there, now closed.
